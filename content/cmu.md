## Disclaimer

In the following reviews, interestingness is judged by the following scale:
- <color val="yellow">1</color>: Not for me
- <color val="yellow">2</color>: I'm glad I learned the content but don't want to go much further
- <color val="yellow">3</color>: This class was interesting, and I would gladly take a followup class
- <color val="yellow">4</color>: This class inspired me to explore the topic on my own
- <color val="yellow">5</color>: This class inspired me to explore the topic on my own, and I consistently enjoyed the lectures and assignments
- <color val="yellow">n+ε</color>: n, and I also particularly enjoyed the coursework/assignments
- <color val="yellow">n-ε</color>: n, but it's probably due more to my interest in the subject than the class itself

As with all opinions, everything I say is very subjective, based very heavily on my personal interests and strengths. Note also that interestingness is not the same thing as how much I enjoyed or got out of the class and that some classes that I didn't rate as very interesting had a huge impact on my development. This also means that I may rate class A as more interesting than class B even if I feel that class B had better instruction and assignments, purely because I found the content of class A more interesting (I've tried to use &pm;&epsilon; to account for this when I think it's a substantial enough factor).

## CS + ECE Classes

### <a name="15122"></a> Principles of Imperative Computation (15122)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 2/5</color>

This class is famously a CMU “weed-out” class for first year CS majors. In my opinion, the toughness of the class comes from figuring out their “point-to proof” scheme: once you get that down, the rest of it is pretty straightforward. I did have a pretty strong math background going in, so this was easier for me than most. Anne Kohlbrenner is a great lecturer, and I highly recommend staying behind after class to ask questions or just hear what she has to say.  As someone who had very little coding experience before coming to college, this class gave me a solid understanding of pointers and memory management, and I quite enjoyed thinking about different data structures and how they’re implemented. I didn’t love the emphasis on provably correct code, but I can understand why it was there. This class is definitely over-hated.

### <a name="15150"></a> Principles of Functional Programming (15150)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 2/5</color>

I found this class quite interesting, since it’s a different way of thinking than normal (imperative) programming. The programming assignments felt like puzzles and actually required you to think, which was nice. Having an early introduction to functional programming, particularly functions as first class values and polymorphism, definitely impacted the way I think about a lot of code, and in hindsight was a great move on CMU's part.

### <a name="15210"></a> Parallel and Sequential Data Structures and Algorithms (15210)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3+ε/5, Difficulty: 3/5</color>

I think 15210 is generally considered to be the hardest of the 152xx cores, and I agree with that assessment. The assignments were challenging and a ton of fun to think through: they definitely made me a lot better at reasoning about algorithms and parallelism. This was also the first time I had to reason about randomized algorithms, and it was extremely satisfying to finally understand why it makes sense to say quicksort has expected time O(n log n). The graph portion of the class served as a great foundation for compilers, and is probably what stuck with me the most just because it's so applicable. 210 is also just a very broad DSA class, and studying for midterms had the added benefit of serving as interview prep.

### <a name="15213"></a> Introduction to Computer Systems (15213)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 4-ε/5, Difficulty: 3/5</color>

This class was my first course in systems. I'd gone into CMU knowing I wanted to learn about how computers work, but this really cemented it for me. I had tons of fun with most assignments, particularly malloclab and shelllab (my one regret is not deleting the starter code for malloc). I didn't love the assignments on networks and concurrency, though the fact that I chose to work on proxylab while on Thanksgiving break instead of enjoying my time with friends probably contributed to that. The textbook for this class is also amazing and I still refer back to it. Overall, this class is probably the most applicable of the cores, and opened my eyes to a ton of things that I find enjoyable. But, in my opinion, the best part of this class is the paradigm shift: having a clearer picture of what's happening under the hood of my laptop and how to take advantage of it.

### <a name="15251"></a> Great Ideas in Theoretical Computer Science (15251)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 2/5</color>

I went into this class with a decent amount of math as my background, so my experience is highly atypical - I didn't find it very hard, and it wasn't as interesting or eye opening as it could have been because I'd been at least mildly exposed to most of the topics before. That being said, I had a ton of fun during the first third of the course with reductions, which I hadn't really seen in much depth before. After reductions, some of the homework assignments began to feel a bit pattern-matchy (again contributing to why I found this class easier than e.g. 15210). There were also a couple expositions that I had pedagogical disagreements with (the class is designed for computer scientests who may or may not be interested in theory, while my pedagogy is aimed much more for mathematicians who want to dabble in theoretical CS - which is probably the wrong one for the class). As someone who has continued to be involved with the course, I also know that both of these gripes have been addressed, and I'd rate the current class as both more interesting and more difficult compared to when I took it.

### <a name="15411"></a> Compiler Design (15411)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 5-ε/5, Difficulty: 5/5</color>

Compilers are cool! I had a ton of fun designing and implementing a compiler in this class. I also learned Rust for this class (as in, my compiler was the first code I wrote in Rust) and had a bunch of fun that way. I'm a bit sad that the focus in this class was largely on implementations that were fully relevant to our assignments, rather than on compilers in general. Topics I wish had been expored more include parsing and parser generators, instruction scheduling, compiling closures and polymorphism, and vectorization optimization. That being said, I learned a lot from this class: how compilers work (obviously), what's happening to my code under the hood when it compiles, and how to make good design decisions when working in a fairly large codebase.

Compilers also had the misfortune of being in the spring, which is when a lot of my extracurriculars get busy. Between extracurriculars and taking tapeout, my partner for the class was also kind of cooked, and so I probably ended up doing a bit more than 50% of the work. This led to some fun consequences such as cramming the entirety of lab 4 into 3 days and working on lab 5 during Dancers' Symposium.

### <a name="18240"></a> Structure and Design of Digital Systems (18240)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 2/5</color>

The winter before this semester, I got annoyed that CMU CS doesn't touch on hardware and decided to take matters into my own hands. At the end of this class, I had a solid basic understanding of how a computer is built out of basic logic gates and how assembly can actually run on a processor. It felt like the analog of 15122 but for hardware by peeling away a layer of abstraction, and so definitely was a bit paradigm-changing for me. Prof. Nace has a bit of a reputation for being hard and unreasonable, but in my experience I found him to be a good lecturer and quite reasonable (he's just also *fair*, you're not going to get special treatment without good reason). I do find myself having some pedagogical disagreements with him, though: he assigns so-called "drill" problems intended to be repetitive practice for the concepts, which I often felt was a waste of my time (the logic being that repetition is necessary for internalizing the ideas, which is true but I personally got a lot more from reasoning about why things were they way they were than from those problems). I also wish that we had spent half a lecture discussing how one actually builds a D flip-flop from combinational circuitry, since doing so demistifies so many things (e.g. how something actually changes on a clock edge, how hold time materializes) that were kind of just asserted.

## Math Classes
### <a name="MSAlg"></a> Mathematical Studies Algebra (21237 + 21238)

<color val="red">Interestingness: N/A, Difficulty: 2.5/5</color>

I went into this class knowing almost all of the content, so any interestingness rating I assign won't be particularly reflective, and my difficulty rating is probably skewed. Prof. Cummings’ homework problems are often computational or focused on a specific example, rather than proving small results, which was quite different from what I’d previously done. As such, I did learn while doing them, but it wasn’t super enjoyable for me. In general, Prof. Cummings’ approach to algebra feels very “hands-on”, which definitely expanded my view. I did enjoy Prof. Cummings’ lectures, and he often had valuable insights that made me feel like I did get something out of the class, even if the bulk of content was review. Prof. Cummings is also an encyclopedia of math, and I learned a lot from every conversation I had with him, whether directly relevant to the class or not.

### <a name="MSAnal"></a> Mathematical Studies Analysis (21269 + 21235 + 21236)

<color val="red">21269 Interestingness: N/A, Difficulty: 3/5</color>

<color val="red">21235 Interestingness: 3.5/5, Difficulty: 4.5/5</color>

<color val="red">21236 Interestingness: 2.5/5, Difficulty: 5/5</color>

I went into 21269 knowing most of the content. MS I covered Lebesgue measure and integration, curves (including the Jordan Curve Theorem), manifolds, and Brower's Fixed Point Theorem. MS II covered differential forms, fixed point theorems with applications to differential equations, and the theory of convex functions. I did enjoy the content of MS I but I tragically just couldn't bring myself to care much about the content of MS II (it was also the same semester as Complex Analysis, Algebraic Topology, and Compilers, all of which I found extremely exciting). It was intrinsically interesting (particularly convex functions), but also quite technical and I wasn't really able to see how it connects to other branches of math. That being said, the point of Math Studies isn't just the content, it's also to give you the skills to do math on your own. The classes definitely succeeded here: I'm a significantly better analyst than 2 years ago. From that POV, this sequence is the most transformative one I've taken at CMU.

Prof. Leoni places a lot of emphasis on "practical" problems, where we actually have to compute integrals or study the behavior of a function. This was great for me, since I would otherwise only look at the theory and not have a clue how it applies to a real example. In general, his approach gave me an appreciation for working through examples, not only to understand why something is true, but also to understand what it is actually saying. His pedagogy is quite different from mine (in particular, he tends to favor sequential characterizations of properties, so a lot of implicit Heine-Borel was happening), which was nice for expanding my view. He's also a very reasonable exam writer, though that didn't stop the entire class from getting humbled on MS II midterm 2, and me from getting humbled again on the final. Overall, I'm extremely glad I got to take this class with him.

### <a name="21318"></a> Analytic Number Theory (21318)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 3/5</color>

Like MS Analysis II, this class suffered from being in the same semester as Complex Analysis, Algebraic Topology, and Compilers, meaning that my potential interest was already drawn thin. The lectures were fun, but at times we became a bit too bogged down in the details for me to really enjoy it. Leo is an excellent lecturer, and did make sure that the big picture was always visible, but the nature of the subject just meant that sometimes the path forward was more symbolic than intuition (I'm sure that there is some intuition that can be gained by doing more analytic number theory and adjacent topics, but I obviously have not (yet) gained that intuition). That being said, I did appreciate the focus on the high-level story, and now have a good mental model for both Dirichlet's Theorem and the Prime Number Theorem.

### <a name="21599"></a> Reading and Research with Prof. Goldmakher (21599)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 4/5, Difficulty: 3/5</color>

This was a reading group under Leo with two of my friends, focusing on combinatorial number theory. We read about discrete Fourier analysis, it's application to prove Roth's Theorem, and recent work by Kelley and Meka about the density of 3-AP-free sets. Actually understanding the whole result was quite technically difficult, and so doing this group significantly improved my ability to come up with intuitive explanations for what complicated results are saying and to interpret equations in context. I'm definitely not the biggest fan of the content we looked at, but forcing myself to wade through and make sense of it greatly improved my mathematical intuition.

### <a name="21623"></a>  Complex Analysis (21623)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 5/5, Difficulty: N/A</color>

Complex analysis is an amazingly beautiful subject, and I enjoyed nearly every second of every lecture of this class. It's just so satisfying seeing how well everything comes together and how powerful the techniques of complex analysis are. Prof. Tkocz is a very good lecturer and his excitement about the results we were proving definitely brought the class together for me. He was also kind enough to let me take the first two midterms, and to return them to me graded. Unfortunately, I stopped showing up to the last quarter of the class as I got busy with Spring Carnival, but this class definitely had some of my favorite lectures at CMU.

### <a name="21701"></a> Discrete Mathematics (21701)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 4+ε/5, Difficulty: 4/5</color>

Florian is hands down one of the best lecturers at CMU, and I highly recommend taking a class with him. He’s quite clear and entertaining, gives historical context and the current state of knowledge that make the lectures more fun, and has very intuitive explanations. The content of this course was extremely interesting: we covered probabilistic combinatorics, Ramsey theory, discrete Fourier analysis, polynomial methods, and Ehrhart theory. There was definitely a topological flavor to a lot of the class (especially using the compactness of the space of colorings *[k]<sup>&Nopf;</sup>*), but a full course in topology wasn’t needed. The homework problems were also challenging but extremely fun, and I actively enjoyed taking the final. This class deepened my interest in both combinatorics and discrete math, and especially in combinatorial number theory.


### <a name="21752"></a> Algebraic Topology (21752)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 5/5, Difficulty: 4/5</color>

Just like 21701, taking this class with Florian was an amazing experience. We focused a lot on the big ideas within the class, which meant that it was really easy to follow the intuition of what was going on, though it also meant that nailing down the details when doing homework became hard. The homework problems were consistently both challenging and interesting, and were probably my favorite assignments of the semester. The content is also extremely beautiful and rich, and I think something definitely worth seeing in your undergrad.

## Other Classes

### <a name="54242"></a> Design for the Stage: Lighting (54242)

<color val="red">Interestingness: 3/5, Difficulty: 2/5</color>

This is a school of drama class paired with the regular *Design for the Stage* course, which I was not enrolled in. That made a couple projects a bit harder (since I hadn't already thought about scripts in the other class), but it wasn't too bad. Mary Ellen is a super inspiring professor, and gives some of the best feedback I've ever receieved. I had fun with most of the assignments, and I think my biggest concrete takeaway from this class was using color in a realistic/theatrical setting. Unfortunately, EOS software doesn't run on linux, which meant that I would have to physically go into Purnell for the programming assignments, which was really inconvenient (I actually ended up dropping *Intro to Lighting Technology* because of this - walking all the way to Purnell on weekends during Pittsburgh winters just wasn't worth it for me), but other than that this class was a really great experience.
